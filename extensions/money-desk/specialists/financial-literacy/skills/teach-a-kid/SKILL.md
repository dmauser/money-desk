## Teach a Kid

description: Age-appropriate money lessons for parents/guardians who want to raise financially literate kids. Five stages — preschool (3-5), early elementary (6-9), late elementary / middle (10-13), high school (14-17), young adult (18-22) — each with the concept that's developmentally right for that age, a concrete activity, and the trap to avoid. Bonus: when to open a custodial Roth IRA for a teen with earned income.

## When to use

- "How do I teach my kid about money?"
- "My teenager just got a job — should I open a Roth IRA for them?"
- "We give our 8-year-old an allowance — are we doing it right?"
- "My college kid keeps overdrawing — how do I help without bailing them out?"

## Inputs needed

- Kid's age (or rough age range).
- What the parent already does (allowance? chores-linked? matching savings?).
- Optional: specific incident or question that prompted this (overdraft, asked for something expensive, first paycheck, etc.).

## The five stages

### Stage 1 — Preschool (ages 3-5): "Money is finite"
- **Developmental concept:** Money exists, it's not infinite, choosing one thing means not choosing another.
- **What sticks at this age:** Counting; physical / visible money; binary "yes / no" choices.
- **Activity:** Three clear jars labeled **SPEND** / **SAVE** / **SHARE**. When the kid gets cash (birthday, etc.), they help split it across the jars. Make the trade-off visible: "If you put it ALL in SPEND, the candy will be gone today; if you put some in SAVE, you can get the bigger thing in a few weeks."
- **Trap to avoid:** Don't make money invisible (cards / apps only). At this age, **physical** matters.

### Stage 2 — Early elementary (ages 6-9): "Earn, wait, choose"
- **Developmental concept:** Earning vs receiving; waiting builds; opportunity cost.
- **What sticks:** Concrete numbers; weekly cycles; small consistent rewards beat irregular big ones.
- **Activity:** Modest allowance ($1-2 per year of age per week is a common benchmark — not gospel). The debate "tie to chores or not?" is real: tying it teaches earning; untying it teaches budgeting independently of behavior. Compromise: small **base** allowance unconditionally (so they have something to budget) + **bonus** for optional extra work (so they learn earning).
- **The compound interest lesson at this age:** Offer to "double" any money they leave untouched in their SAVE jar for one full month. Make it visible. They'll feel the magic before they understand the math.
- **Trap to avoid:** Bailing out a spending mistake. Let them feel the regret of having spent their allowance on day 1 — that's the lesson. (Within reason.)

### Stage 3 — Late elementary / middle (ages 10-13): "Goals, tracking, planning"
- **Developmental concept:** Goals require planning. Income and expenses are abstract numbers in a ledger.
- **What sticks:** Phone apps; their own debit card / pre-paid card; spreadsheets if they're the type.
- **Activity:** Open a kids' checking + debit account (Greenlight / Step / Capital One Money / Chase First Banking). Migrate allowance to the card. Teach the kid to check their balance before spending — this single habit is the most-skipped adult skill.
- **First "investing" intro:** Show them YOUR brokerage / 401(k) (or a paper portfolio) — "this is what 'putting money to work' looks like". Use a $100 paper allocation across 3-4 stocks they recognize (Nintendo, Disney, Apple, whatever) and check it together monthly. The first time they see -8% is more educational than any lecture.
- **Trap to avoid:** Turning every transaction into a teachable moment. Sometimes kids just want to buy the slime. Save the lessons for moments they're asking.

### Stage 4 — High school (14-17): "Earn for real, taxes exist"
- **Developmental concept:** Real income, real taxes, real bank accounts, real consequences. Compound interest as a math object.
- **What sticks:** Their own pay stub from a real job; their own checking account; the realization that "$15/hour" doesn't mean "$15 × hours" in your pocket.
- **Activity:**
  1. **Paycheck walkthrough.** First pay stub from their first job → walk through FIT / FICA / Medicare line by line (or run **Paycheck Walkthrough** in Financial Literacy together).
  2. **Custodial Roth IRA.** The day they have earned income, you (the parent) can open a **Custodial Roth IRA** for them. Contribute up to the lesser of their earned income or the annual limit (`md_facts` → `irs_contribution_limits.ira_traditional_or_roth`). $3,000 contributed at age 16 and left until age 67 is roughly $58,600 in today's dollars at the `md_facts` 6% real return (3,000 × 1.06^51). This is the single highest-leverage move available to a teenager and almost nobody does it.
  3. **Credit literacy.** Add them as an authorized user on YOUR oldest credit card with the lowest utilization. They build credit history (oldest line on their report ages with yours) without needing to actually use the card. They don't need to know the card number.
  4. **The car / phone decision.** First big purchase decision — let them OWN it. "Here's what you have; here's what each option costs; you decide." Even if they pick poorly.
- **Trap to avoid:** Co-signing anything. Co-signing a car loan or apartment with your teen is YOUR debt with their behavior; almost always a mistake. Authorized-user on a credit card is different — no liability to you.

### Stage 5 — Young adult (18-22): "On your own (kind of)"
- **Developmental concept:** Independent financial identity. The transition from "the family covers it" to "this is your problem".
- **What sticks:** Their own credit score; their own 401(k) at the first real job; their own rent payments.
- **Activity:**
  1. **Open their own credit card.** Student card or secured card if no income. The earlier the age of credit, the better the score for life.
  2. **First 401(k) at first real job:** AT LEAST the match. Match math is identical for a 22-year-old and a 55-year-old, but the 22-year-old has 40 more years of compounding.
  3. **Continue Roth IRA contributions** if they have earned income — flip the custodial Roth IRA to their own name at age of majority (18 or 21 depending on state).
  4. **The family safety-net conversation.** Be explicit about what you WILL and WON'T bail out. Vague safety nets cause the worst money behavior — clear ones cause the best. ("I'll help if you lose your job and can't make rent. I won't help if you put car repairs on a credit card you could have saved for.")
- **Trap to avoid:** Continuing to subsidize lifestyle past graduation. The longer the subsidy lasts, the harder the eventual landing. Define an end date for any post-college support.

## Bonus: the custodial Roth IRA (worth its own callout)

If a minor has **earned income** (W-2, 1099, paid tutoring, paid babysitting, lifeguarding, etc.), you as a parent can open a **Custodial Roth IRA** and contribute up to the lesser of their earned income or the annual limit.

- **Why it's the highest-leverage move available:** 50+ years of tax-free compounding from contributions made at ages 14-22.
- **Worked example:** $3,000 contributed at age 16, left untouched at the `md_facts` 6% real return, becomes roughly **$58,600** in today's dollars at age 67 (3,000 × 1.06^51).
- **Where to open:** Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard all offer custodial Roth IRAs at no cost.
- **The catch:** The kid needs documented earned income. Allowance / gift money / chore money doesn't count. A real W-2 or self-employment income with a record (Venmo for tutoring, etc.) does.
- **Money for the contribution can come from you (the parent)** — the IRS only requires that the kid had the earned income, not that they personally fund the contribution. Many parents match: "every dollar you earn, I'll put in your Roth IRA."
- **At age of majority (18 or 21 by state), the account converts to a regular Roth IRA in their name.** They CAN withdraw it; the hope is the conversation at age 18 about what's sitting there means they don't.

### Calculation method

1. **Stage** by `kid_age` (Decision rules table).
2. **Activity this month** = the first activity listed for that stage that is not already in `current_practices`; if all are in place, use the stage's last activity.
3. **Trap** = the stage's "Trap to avoid", verbatim in substance.
4. **Preview** = the next stage's developmental concept (Stage 5 → "Not applicable — final stage").
5. **Custodial Roth** (only if `kid_earned_income_annual_usd` > 0): max contribution = `min(kid_earned_income_annual_usd, ira_traditional_or_roth)`. Projection: `FV = contribution × (1 + r)^(67 − kid_age)`, r from md_facts; round to the nearest $100. Worked example in the text uses $3,000 at age 16 with the same formula (at r = 0.06: ≈ $58,600 in today's dollars).
6. If `trigger_incident` is present, open "What lands at this age" with one sentence that connects the incident to the stage concept.
7. Length: 250–450 words total, plain language, no jargon without an inline definition.
